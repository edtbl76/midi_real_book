# Ensemble: Brothers Johnson / Quincy Jones

## Context

This ensemble draws together three distinct funk lineages: Louis Johnson and Wah Wah Watson as core Brothers Johnson / Quincy Jones collaborators, Greg Phillinganes and Jerry Hey as Quincy Jones production regulars who also defined the sound of Michael Jackson's peak albums, and Jimmy Nolen and Clyde Stubblefield as the James Brown rhythm section who invented the foundational vocabulary that made all of it possible. The result is a cross-section of late-1970s and early-1980s funk at its most refined — slap bass, wah-drenched rhythm guitar, sophisticated horn arrangements, and the chicken-scratch rhythm guitar and polyrhythmic drumming that James Brown's band perfected a decade earlier and the rest of the genre never stopped borrowing from.

## Louis Johnson

Louis Johnson (1955–2015), known as "Thunder Thumbs," was one half of The Brothers Johnson alongside his brother George Johnson and is widely credited with pioneering slap bass technique in pop and R&B. His aggressive thumb-driven attack on the Music Man StingRay — he was among the first prominent players to use one — gave Quincy Jones productions a low-end signature that remained immediately identifiable across the late 1970s and into the 1980s. He also played bass on several tracks from Michael Jackson's *Off the Wall* (1979), extending his reach well beyond the Brothers Johnson catalog.

## Wah Wah Watson

Wah Wah Watson (born Melvin Ragin, 1950) is a session guitarist whose name describes his method: his wah-pedal work was so central to his identity that it became the name everyone used. He was a fixture on Motown recordings in the early 1970s and became one of Quincy Jones's most trusted studio guitarists, appearing on Brothers Johnson albums and Stevie Wonder sessions alike. His approach prioritizes rhythmic precision over flash — the wah and the groove are one continuous idea.

## Clyde Stubblefield

Clyde Stubblefield (1943–2017) was James Brown's primary drummer from 1965 to approximately 1971, and the creator of what became the most sampled drum break in recorded history — the "Funky Drummer" break from 1969. His playing was characterized by extreme polyrhythmic independence: the hi-hat, snare, and kick did not simply duplicate each other but created interlocking patterns that made the groove feel simultaneously tight and alive. Along with Jimmy Nolen, he defined the rhythmic architecture that all subsequent funk built on top of.

## Greg Phillinganes

Greg Phillinganes (born 1956) is one of the most recorded session and touring keyboardists of the pop era. He became a core Quincy Jones collaborator beginning in the late 1970s and played on all three of Michael Jackson's major Jones-produced albums — *Off the Wall*, *Thriller*, and *Bad*. He also had a long association with Stevie Wonder and spent two decades in Eric Clapton's touring band. His keyboard work synthesizes the harmonic depth of soul and R&B with the rhythmic precision required by funk production.

## Jerry Hey

Jerry Hey (born 1947) is one of the most recorded session trumpeters and brass arrangers in pop history. He became a cornerstone of Quincy Jones's production sound and brought the same approach to George Benson, Earth Wind & Fire, and Michael Jackson records. His arrangements are distinguished by tight brass voicings that serve the groove — staccato stabs in the right places, held chords that build tension, and solo lines that are melodically memorable without overstepping the arrangement.
