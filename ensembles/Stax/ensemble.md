# Ensemble: Stax

## Context

This ensemble draws from two dominant schools of 1960s American soul: the Motown Funk Brothers (James Jamerson, Benny Benjamin, Earl Van Dyke, Jack Ashford, Dennis Coffey) who built the Detroit pop-soul machine at Hitsville U.S.A., and Stax's Steve Cropper (Memphis), whose grittier, church-rooted guitar defined the Stax/Volt sound. Together these players represent the two poles of 1960s R&B rhythm-section craft — Detroit's polished, orchestrated groove against Memphis's loose, spontaneous earthiness.

## James Jamerson

James Jamerson was the primary bassist of the Motown Funk Brothers, the uncredited Detroit session band behind Hitsville U.S.A. His melodic, walking bass lines — improvised in the studio, often built on a single finger — redefined what a bass player could do in popular music. He played flatwound strings on a detuned 1962 Fender Precision Bass he called "the Funk," and his chromatic vocabulary and rhythmic independence underneath locked-in drum grooves remain the benchmark for soul bass playing.

## Dennis Coffey

Dennis Coffey was a Detroit-based session guitarist who joined the Motown Funk Brothers in the late 1960s and introduced the wah-wah pedal to the label's recordings over initial resistance. His entry coincided with Motown's shift toward harder, more psychedelic funk under producer Norman Whitfield. He also pursued a parallel solo career and scored a top-10 hit with the instrumental "Scorpio" in 1971.

## Steve Cropper

Steve Cropper is the guitarist and co-founder of Booker T. & the M.G.'s, the house band at Stax Records in Memphis. His playing is the defining sound of Memphis soul: spare, rhythmically precise, built on short staccato chords and economical single-note lines. He co-wrote many of the songs he played on, including "(Sittin' On) The Dock of the Bay" with Otis Redding and "In the Midnight Hour" with Wilson Pickett. He plays a 1961 Fender Telecaster.

## Benny Benjamin

Benny Benjamin was the primary drummer of the Motown Funk Brothers in the label's early years and the architect of the foundational Motown rhythmic feel: slightly behind the beat, heavy on the backbeat, with a loose, swinging quality that gave even the most polished arrangements a human center of gravity. He died on April 20, 1969, before Motown moved to Los Angeles, so his recorded work is concentrated in the Detroit years.

## Earl Van Dyke

Earl Van Dyke was the musical director and primary pianist and organist of the Motown Funk Brothers, leading the band at Hitsville U.S.A. through most of the label's Detroit era. He organized rehearsals, coordinated the rhythm section, and served as the harmonic anchor of hundreds of sessions. His keyboard work ranges from propulsive gospel-inflected piano comping to sustained Hammond organ pads that thicken the Motown sound without drawing attention to themselves.

## Jack Ashford

Jack Ashford was the primary percussionist of the Motown Funk Brothers, responsible for the tambourine sound that became one of the most recognizable sonic signatures of the Motown recordings. His syncopated tambourine hits — bright, crisp, and rhythmically independent from the drum kit — added a high-frequency rhythmic layer that helped the records translate to small speakers and AM radio. He also played vibraphone on many sessions.
