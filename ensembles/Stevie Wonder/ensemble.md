# Ensemble: Stevie Wonder

## Context

Stevie Wonder emerged from Motown as a child prodigy in the early 1960s and transformed himself into one of popular music's most complete artists during the 1972–1976 "classic period" — a run of five consecutive albums (Music of My Mind, Talking Book, Innervisions, Fulfillingness' First Finale, and Songs in the Key of Life) that earned him three consecutive Grammy Album of the Year awards. Working closely with producers Robert Margouleff and Malcolm Cecil and their TONTO modular synthesizer, Wonder played most instruments himself in the studio — Fender Rhodes, Clavinet, Moog and ARP synthesizers, harmonica, drums, and bass — while also drawing on a tight circle of session players and his live band Wonderlove. The players listed here represent the extended ensemble that shaped his studio and touring sound from the mid-1970s onward.

## Nathan Watts

Nathan Lamar Watts was born March 25, 1954, in Detroit, Michigan, and began his career on trumpet before switching to bass after graduating high school in 1972 — a decision encouraged by childhood friend Ray Parker Jr. In 1974, following a recommendation from Parker, Watts auditioned for Stevie Wonder and replaced Reggie McBride as Wonder's full-time bassist. He contributed to landmark albums including Songs in the Key of Life (1976), Hotter Than July (1981), and Original Musiquarium I (1982), and has served as Wonder's musical director since 1994. His pocket-oriented, gospel-rooted style — marked by precise note length, melodic fills, and deep groove authority — became a defining element of Wonder's sound for more than four decades. Beyond Wonder, his credits include sessions for Diana Ross, the Jacksons, Lionel Richie, Herbie Hancock, and Paul McCartney, among many others.

## Michael Sembello

Michael Andrew Sembello was born April 17, 1954, in Ardmore, Pennsylvania, and studied with jazz guitarist Pat Martino — training that gave him the harmonic vocabulary to navigate both jazz and studio pop. By age 17 he was working professionally, and by 1974 he was contributing guitar to Stevie Wonder's Fulfillingness' First Finale; he continued on Songs in the Key of Life (1976), where he was credited as lead and rhythm guitarist on most tracks, played the intricate jazz-rock solo on the instrumental "Contusion," and co-wrote "Saturn" with Wonder. His broader session credits encompass Michael Jackson, Diana Ross, Chaka Khan, George Benson, David Sanborn, and many others. As a solo artist, he is best known for "Maniac" (1983), co-written with Dennis Matkosky for the Flashdance soundtrack, which reached number one in the United States and earned him Academy Award and Golden Globe nominations.

## John Robinson

John Frederick Robinson — known professionally as JR — was born December 29, 1954, and has been called one of the most recorded drummers in history, with credits on more than 10,000 sessions and over 50 Grammy-winning recordings. He came to national attention in 1978 after Rufus and Chaka Khan recruited him from a club band outside Cleveland, and his work on their album Street Player launched his career as an A-list session player in Los Angeles. Through his long collaboration with producer Quincy Jones, Robinson played on Michael Jackson's Off the Wall (1979) and anchored the rhythm tracks for the charity single "We Are the World" (1985). His hits as a session drummer span Lionel Richie's "All Night Long," the Pointer Sisters' "I'm So Excited," and Steve Winwood's "Higher Love," reflecting a versatility across soul, funk, and pop that made him a default choice for major productions throughout the 1980s and beyond. He won a Grammy for the Rufus and Chaka Khan single "Ain't Nobody" and has appeared on more than 30 number-one pop records.

## Stevie Wonder

Stevie Wonder (born Stevland Hardaway Morris, May 13, 1950, in Saginaw, Michigan) signed with Motown's Tamla label at age 11 and had his first number-one hit, "Fingertips – Pt. 2," at 13. After renegotiating his contract at 21, he gained full creative control and launched his "classic period" — five consecutive landmark albums between 1972 and 1976 that made him the first artist to win the Grammy Album of the Year award with three consecutive releases. A multi-instrumentalist who recorded most studio parts himself — piano, Fender Rhodes, Clavinet, Moog and ARP synthesizers, harmonica, drums, and bass — Wonder collaborated closely with producers Robert Margouleff and Malcolm Cecil and their TONTO synthesizer on his early 1970s records before assembling the sprawling Songs in the Key of Life (1976) with a larger cast of players. His harmonica playing is as distinctive as his keyboard work, rooted in the diatonic blues vocabulary but extended into chromatic jazz phrasing; the Hohner 64 Chromonica has been his primary harmonica instrument.

## Toots Thielemans

Jean-Baptiste Frédéric Isidor, Baron Thielemans (April 29, 1922 – August 22, 2016), known as Toots Thielemans, was a Belgian jazz musician and the preeminent jazz chromatic harmonica player of the twentieth century. Born in Brussels, he came to international attention playing with Benny Goodman's band during its 1949–1950 European tour, emigrated to the United States in 1951, and spent six years with the George Shearing Quintet from 1953. He is best known outside jazz circles for the haunting harmonica theme he performed for John Barry's score to the film Midnight Cowboy (1969), and for his own composition "Bluesette" (1961), which has been covered by more than a hundred artists. A longtime collaborator with Bill Evans, Quincy Jones, and Paul Simon — he played harmonica on Simon's "Night Game" from Still Crazy After All These Years (1975) — Thielemans was also closely associated with Stevie Wonder, performing together at the 1999 Polar Music Prize ceremony in Stockholm. Hohner produced two signature harmonica models to his specifications: the Mellow Tone and the Hard Bopper, both 12-hole chromatic instruments.
