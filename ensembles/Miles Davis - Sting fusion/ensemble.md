# Ensemble: Miles Davis / Sting fusion

## Context

In the early-to-mid 1980s, a tight circle of jazz-trained New York musicians appeared on both Miles Davis's electric comeback records and Sting's debut solo album, making them the clearest bridge between jazz and mainstream pop of that era. Darryl Jones, Omar Hakim, Kenny Kirkland, and Branford Marsalis all played in Miles Davis's electric band and then moved directly into Sting's "Dream of the Blue Turtles" group in 1985; Mike Stern appeared as Miles's guitarist from 1981 to 1983 and again in 1985. The result is a pool of players who understood how to hold a groove, improvise inside a pop structure, and bring jazz harmonic language to an audience that had never heard it that way before.

## Darryl Jones

Darryl Jones (b. 1961, Chicago) joined Miles Davis's electric band in 1983, replacing Marcus Miller, and stayed through the "Decoy" and "You're Under Arrest" sessions. In 1985 he moved to Sting's "Dream of the Blue Turtles" band alongside Kirkland, Hakim, and Marsalis, appearing on the album and the Bring on the Night concert film. Since 1994 he has been the Rolling Stones' touring bassist, a position he has held for every subsequent tour without becoming an official band member. His sound sits between the articulate funk of his Miles years and the rock pocket the Stones require — controlled dynamics, clean attacks, and a consistent sense of where the one is.

## Mike Stern

Mike Stern (b. 1953, Boston) was the guitarist in Miles Davis's electric band from 1981 to 1983 and again in 1985. He came to Miles after stints with Blood, Sweat & Tears and Billy Cobham, and his aggressive, bending, wailing tone — rooted in Hendrix and Wes Montgomery simultaneously — was an immediate contrast to the smoother textures Miles had used in the late 1970s. After leaving Miles's band he built a solo career in jazz fusion, recording prolifically for Atlantic and later ESC Records. He is among the most technically demanding guitarists to emerge from the mid-1980s jazz-fusion scene, and the Miles period recordings remain the best introduction to what his playing sounds like under real pressure.

## Omar Hakim

Omar Hakim (b. 1959, New York) is one of the most recorded drummers of the 1980s. He was in Miles Davis's electric band from 1983 to 1985 and simultaneously became a first-call studio drummer across pop, rock, and jazz. He played on David Bowie's "Let's Dance" (1983), replaced Peter Erskine in Weather Report for "Sportin' Life" (1985), and was the drummer on Dire Straits' "Brothers in Arms" (1985) — all in the same eighteen-month window as the Miles and Sting work. His kit work is characterized by a deep pocket, clean ghost notes, and a studio-ready sonic clarity that made him unusually versatile across contexts that most jazz drummers could not navigate.

## Kenny Kirkland

Kenny Kirkland (1955–1998) was the pianist for Sting's Dream of the Blue Turtles band and one of the most harmonically sophisticated players of his generation. He studied at the Manhattan School of Music and worked briefly with Miles Davis before the Sting association. After leaving Sting's group he became the anchor of the Branford Marsalis Quartet from 1986 to 1994 and recorded one acclaimed solo album for GRP in 1991. His playing drew on Bill Evans, Herbie Hancock, and McCoy Tyner without sounding like a synthesis of any of them — it had a distinctive rhythmic urgency and a voicing vocabulary that made even standard progressions sound fresh. He died on November 12, 1998.

## Branford Marsalis

Branford Marsalis (b. 1960, Breaux Bridge, Louisiana) is the eldest of the Marsalis brothers and the most genre-crossing. He studied at Berklee and with Blakey's Jazz Messengers before joining Sting's 1985 band, a move that drew criticism from some jazz purists but introduced his playing to a mass audience. He led his own quartet through the late 1980s and 1990s, served as musical director and house bandleader for The Tonight Show with Jay Leno from 1992 to 1995, and has continued recording under his own name since. His primary instruments are soprano and tenor saxophone; his tone on soprano is immediately recognizable — wide and slightly reedy, with a vibrato that sits between Wayne Shorter and John Coltrane.
