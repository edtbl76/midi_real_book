# Ensemble: The Who

## Context

Formed in London in 1964, The Who became one of the defining forces of hard rock and the rock concept album, pioneering the form with Tommy (1969) and Quadrophenia (1973). The engine of the band was the rhythm section: Keith Moon's explosive, melodically-driven drumming — never keeping time so much as launching the music — and John Entwistle's trebly, lead-bass counterpoint that turned the bottom end into a third solo voice. Above them, Pete Townshend's windmill power-chord attacks and open-string drones drove a physical intensity few rhythm guitarists have matched, while Roger Daltrey's arena-sized voice gave the songs their emotional center and narrative force.

## John Entwistle

John Entwistle (1944–2002) grew up in Chiswick, west London, and was playing in skiffle and trad-jazz groups by his mid-teens before joining the Detours, who became The Who. Where most rock bassists locked in with the kick drum and stayed out of the way, Entwistle played a high-treble, distorted lead bass that functioned as a third melodic voice — countermelodies and fills wove around Townshend's guitar rather than simply supporting it. His right-hand technique, sometimes called the "typewriter" style, produced a percussive attack that cut through enormous stage volume without a pick. Beyond The Who he composed quirky, sardonic songs ("My Wife," "Boris the Spider") and released a string of solo albums beginning with Smash Your Head Against the Wall (1971). He remained the band's sonic anchor through every lineup change until his death in Las Vegas in 2002, the night before a scheduled North American tour.

## Pete Townshend

Pete Townshend (born 1945, Chiswick, London) is The Who's principal songwriter, rhythm guitarist, and de facto musical architect, responsible for nearly the entire catalogue from 1965 onward. His playing is built around two complementary instincts: the kinetic violence of the windmill strum — arm swinging in a full arc before crashing into open chords — and a harmonic sophistication drawn from Townshend's study of Meher Baba's teachings, which shaped the open-tuning and suspended-chord language of tracks like "Baba O'Riley" and "Behind Blue Eyes." He pioneered the use of synthesizer loops in rock (the arpeggiated Lowrey organ on "Baba O'Riley," the ARP synthesizer sequences on "Won't Get Fooled Again") years before they became standard. His guitar feedback experiments at the Marquee Club and Marquee-era live shows helped define the sonic vocabulary of hard rock and heavily influenced Jimi Hendrix. Outside The Who, Townshend released the solo concept albums Empty Glass (1980) and White City: A Novel (1985), and wrote the book on Pete Townshend: Who Am I (2012).

## Keith Moon

Keith Moon (1946–1978) was born in Wembley, Middlesex, and joined The Who in 1964, replacing Doug Sandom after an audition at which he reportedly destroyed the drum kit to prove a point. His technique was unlike any drummer of the era: no conventional ride-cymbal timekeeping, no standard backbeat as a fixed anchor, but instead a continuous melodic improvisation across all drums simultaneously, driven by a thunderous right foot and an instinct for ensemble conversation rather than metronomic precision. He regarded fills not as decoration between beats but as the primary statement, with the "in-between" time fitted around them. He was the principal architect of the tom-heavy, polyrhythmic style that became the benchmark for hard rock drumming, and his ability to convey dynamic control and raw excitement simultaneously within a single song section remains virtually unmatched. Moon died in London in September 1978 from an accidental overdose of Heminevrin tablets taken to suppress alcohol withdrawal.

## Roger Daltrey

Roger Daltrey (born 1944, Hammersmith, London) was a founding member of The Who and its lead vocalist throughout the band's entire career. As a teenager he built the first Detours guitar from scratch in a school workshop, making him originally a guitarist before ceding that role to Townshend. His voice is a physically powerful lyric tenor capable of both the chest-voice aggression needed to cut through Moon and Entwistle at full volume and a surprising delicacy in intimate passages — qualities that made him ideal for the vocal-storytelling demands of Tommy and Quadrophenia. His microphone technique became iconic: spinning the mic cable in wide circles overhead before snapping it back for the downbeat, a move born partly from practical necessity (avoiding feedback on stage) and partly from pure theatrical instinct. Beyond The Who he pursued a parallel acting career — most notably the title role in Ken Russell's film of Tommy (1975) and Liszt in Lisztomania (1975) — and released several solo albums, including a well-regarded collaboration with Leo Sayer (Daltrey, 1973).
