# Ensemble: Cream

## Context

Cream was a British power trio active from 1966 to 1968, comprising guitarist Eric Clapton, bassist and vocalist Jack Bruce, and drummer Ginger Baker. They were among the first rock acts to build entire performances around extended blues-based improvisation, and their three-way conversation — Clapton's pentatonic lead work, Bruce's melodic and contrapuntal bass lines, and Baker's jazz-inflected two-bass-drum kit — defined the template for the blues-rock power trio. This ensemble also includes Robin Trower (who collaborated with Jack Bruce in BLT) and Gary Moore (who formed BBM with Bruce and Baker in 1993–94); their key recordings are drawn from their own solo catalogs, which share the same blues-rock trio vocabulary.

## Jack Bruce

Jack Bruce (1943–2014) was a Scottish bassist, vocalist, and composer who studied cello and double bass at the Royal Scottish Academy of Music before moving into professional jazz and R&B in the early 1960s. After stints with the Graham Bond Organisation (where he first played alongside Ginger Baker) and briefly with Manfred Mann, he co-founded Cream in 1966, becoming the group's primary lead vocalist and the co-writer — with lyricist Pete Brown — of most of their classic material, including "Sunshine of Your Love," "White Room," and "I Feel Free." His defining technical trait was treating the bass as a melodic instrument equal to the guitar: dense, contrapuntal lines played on a short-scale Gibson EB-3 through overdriven Marshall Super Bass stacks. After Cream he recorded a sequence of solo albums beginning with Songs for a Tailor (1969), collaborated with Robin Trower and Bill Lordan in BLT (1981), and later formed the power-trio BBM with Gary Moore and Ginger Baker (1993–94).

## Eric Clapton

Eric Clapton (born 1945) came up through the Yardbirds and John Mayall's Bluesbreakers, where his Les Paul-through-Marshall sound earned him a devoted blues-purist following before Cream ever formed. In Cream (1966–68) he developed the "woman tone" — rolling the guitar's tone control fully back through a cranked Marshall to produce a singing, vocal-like sustain — and matched Jack Bruce's melodic aggression with melodic aggression of his own. His Cream-era rig centered on a 1964 Gibson SG Standard (the psychedelically painted "Fool" guitar), a 1964 ES-335 TDC, and 100-watt Marshall JTM45/100 heads through 4×12 cabinets, with a Vox wah-wah as his only effect in live settings. The nickname "Slowhand," coined by a promoter, reflected his deliberate, tone-obsessed approach: where other players added notes, Clapton bent and sustained the ones he chose.

## Robin Trower

Robin Trower (born 1945) grew up in Southend-on-Sea and first came to prominence with Procol Harum from 1967 to 1971, appearing on their first five albums, before leaving to lead his own power trio. His defining sound — Fender Stratocaster through Marshall 100-watt heads with a Uni-Vibe for chorus and vibrato — drew immediate comparisons to Jimi Hendrix, whom Trower has cited as his primary influence, though his phrasing is distinctively darker and more methodical. His 1974 album Bridge of Sighs broke through commercially in the United States and remains his best-known work, with songs like "Too Rolling Stoned" and the title track becoming live staples; he released four consecutive gold-certified albums between 1974 and 1977. In the early 1980s he began a long collaborative relationship with Jack Bruce, recording BLT (1981) and Truce (1982) with Bruce and drummer Bill Lordan, and later Seven Moons (2007) with Bruce and Gary Husband.

## Gary Moore

Gary Moore (1952–2011) was born in Belfast and moved to Dublin at sixteen to join Skid Row, establishing himself as an unusually powerful blues-rock guitarist in his teens. He had multiple stints with Thin Lizzy — most significantly recording the album Black Rose: A Rock Legend (1979) — before turning to a sustained solo career that ranged from hard rock (Corridors of Power, 1982) through melodic rock (Wild Frontier, 1987) to a full blues pivot with Still Got the Blues (1990), his best-selling album, which brought him collaborations with Albert King, Albert Collins, and George Harrison. His primary instrument for much of his solo career was a 1959 Gibson Les Paul Standard nicknamed "Greeny," previously owned by Peter Green of Fleetwood Mac, whose reversed neck pickup gave the guitar its distinctive hollow, nasal sound; he later favoured another 1959 Les Paul nicknamed "Stripe." In 1993 he joined Jack Bruce and Ginger Baker to form BBM, releasing Around the Next Dream (1994), a deliberate return to the power-trio blues-rock format of Cream.

## Ginger Baker

Ginger Baker (1939–2019) was born in Lewisham, London, and developed his drumming through jazz — specifically through bebop's syncopation and ride-cymbal vocabulary, and later through African rhythmic patterns he studied under Phil Seamen and would later explore in Nigeria, where he built the continent's first modern recording studio after Cream disbanded. He was one of the first rock drummers to use two bass drums alternately, an approach he began after watching Duke Ellington's drummer Sam Woodyard in 1966. His Cream-era kit was a Ludwig Silver Sparkle double-bass setup (22" and 20" kicks, 12" and 13" rack toms, 14" and 16" floor toms) with a 14" Leedy Broadway snare, played with the same Zildjian cymbals — including a 22" riveted ride and 14" hi-hats — for his entire career. The five-minute "Toad" on Fresh Cream (1966) is widely cited as rock's first extended drum solo.
