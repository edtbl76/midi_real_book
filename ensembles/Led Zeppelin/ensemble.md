# Ensemble: Led Zeppelin

## Context

Led Zeppelin formed in London in 1968 and recorded nine studio albums over twelve years before disbanding after drummer John Bonham's death in September 1980. Working across hard rock, blues-rock, folk, and Eastern-influenced music, they collapsed genre categories in ways few bands have matched — moving from acoustic Appalachian fingerpicking to crushing riffs to orchestrated epics within a single album. What makes their ensemble interplay distinctive is the three-way conversation between Bonham's enormous, groove-locked drumming, John Paul Jones's bass and keyboard arrangements (rooted in years of professional session and arranging work), and Jimmy Page's layered studio production — a rhythm section that could hold enormous dynamic range while Page built textures on top.

## John Paul Jones

John Paul Jones (born John Baldwin, 3 January 1946, Sidcup, Kent) spent the first half of the 1960s as one of London's most in-demand session bassists and string arrangers — his credits include arranging the Rolling Stones' "She's a Rainbow" and working on Donovan sessions alongside Jimmy Page. That background gives his playing an unusual dual character: he can lock into a pure, unadorned groove (his signature on "When the Levee Breaks" and "Immigrant Song") but also reshape the harmonic texture of a song entirely through keyboard writing, as he does with the Mellotron on "The Rain Song" and the Clavinet on "Trampled Under Foot." His own bass playing is notable for use of octave patterns, picked attack for heavy articulation, and an almost orchestral sense of where the low end should sit against the drums — he and Bonham operated as a unit, with Jones often commenting that the goal was for bass and drums to sound like a single instrument.

## Jimmy Page

Jimmy Page (born James Patrick Page, 9 January 1944, Heston, Middlesex) was by the mid-1960s one of the most sought-after session guitarists in Britain, recording with the Kinks, the Who, Donovan, and many others before joining the Yardbirds in 1966. When the Yardbirds dissolved in 1968 he founded Led Zeppelin, serving simultaneously as the band's lead guitarist, producer, and primary architect of their studio sound — a role that made him unusual among rock guitarists of the era. He is associated with several distinct techniques: violin bow guitar (used live and in the studio on "Dazed and Confused"), DADGAD and open-D tuning for drone-based riff writing, layered guitar overdubs (12 separate guitar parts on "Achilles Last Stand"), and a loose, blues-inflected lead style that varies significantly from track to track. After Led Zeppelin he released two albums with Paul Rodgers's band the Firm (1985–86), a solo album (*Outrider*, 1988), the *Coverdale/Page* album (1993), and reunited with Robert Plant for *No Quarter: Unledded* (1994).

## John Bonham

John Henry Bonham (31 May 1948 – 25 September 1980, Redditch, Worcestershire) played with Robert Plant in Band of Joy from 1967 to 1968 before being recruited to what became Led Zeppelin — the band was effectively his entire professional career. He died at age 32, and Led Zeppelin disbanded immediately after his death. Bonham's playing is characterised by extreme dynamic range, a loud and consistent rimshot backbeat, and a single bass drum technique — he never used a double pedal — capable of producing triplet figures fast enough that listeners assumed otherwise; his famous bass drum triplets on "Good Times Bad Times" remain a benchmark study for single-pedal technique. His influences were jazz: Buddy Rich, Max Roach, and Gene Krupa shaped his sense of time and his use of hand-played tom passages. By 1973 he had adopted the Ludwig amber Vistalite acrylic kit with Paiste 2002 cymbals, the setup associated with the band's later recordings.
