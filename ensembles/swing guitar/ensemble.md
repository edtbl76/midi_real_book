# Ensemble: swing guitar

## Context

The Oscar Peterson Trio with Ray Brown on bass represents one of the defining acoustic jazz ensembles of the postwar era — a model of how piano, bass, and drums can achieve maximum swing with maximum musical communication. Joe Pass and Barney Kessel were leading jazz guitarists in this world, both emerging from the bebop vocabulary and developing into complete musicians across solo, duo, and ensemble contexts. Buddy Rich and Dizzy Gillespie represent the big-band and bebop pillars of swing — Gillespie as bebop's trumpet architect, Rich as the most technically spectacular drummer of his generation.

## Ray Brown

Ray Brown was one of the great bassists of the twentieth century, equally renowned for his walking bass lines, his tone, and his ability to swing with any player at any tempo. He was Dizzy Gillespie's bassist in the 1940s bebop era, married Ella Fitzgerald, and then spent the central decades of his career as the anchor of the Oscar Peterson Trio (1951-1966). After leaving Peterson, Brown led his own trio and worked as a session musician in Los Angeles until his death in 2002.

## Barney Kessel

Barney Kessel was an Oklahoma-born guitarist who became one of jazz's most versatile and in-demand players from the late 1940s through the 1970s. He won the *Downbeat* poll multiple times, appeared in the jazz film *Jammin' the Blues* (1944), and worked as a Hollywood session musician while maintaining an active jazz career. Kessel's playing combines bebop fluency with blues earthiness and a rhythmic swing feel that made him equally effective as a soloist and as an accompanist. He was a founding member of The Poll Winners with Ray Brown and Shelly Manne.

## Buddy Rich

Buddy Rich was widely considered the most technically proficient drummer who ever lived, combining extraordinary speed (he practiced no rudiments beyond what he learned as a child performer) with a powerhouse musical intuition and an aggressive swing feel that made his big band one of the most exciting live acts in jazz. Rich worked with Artie Shaw, Tommy Dorsey, and led his own bands across multiple decades. His legendary performances with his big band in the 1960s and 1970s are documented on film and are among the most viscerally exciting jazz performances recorded.

## Oscar Peterson

Oscar Peterson was a Canadian pianist widely considered one of the greatest jazz pianists who ever lived. His technical facility was extraordinary — he could play single-note bebop lines at any tempo with complete clarity — but his playing was equally remarkable for its blues feeling, swing authority, and the joy that came through in everything he played. Peterson led his own trios throughout his career (with different configurations of guitar, bass, and drums), recorded prolifically for the Verve and Pablo labels, and played with virtually every major jazz musician of his era.

## Dizzy Gillespie

Dizzy Gillespie was one of bebop's founding fathers — along with Charlie Parker — and was the trumpeter who most directly shaped the language of modern jazz trumpet. His tone was brilliant and penetrating, his range was extraordinary, and his harmonic sophistication (he contributed to the theoretical framework of bebop harmony) made him as important as a thinker as a player. Gillespie also brought Afro-Cuban rhythms into jazz, hiring Chano Pozo in 1947 and creating a Latin-jazz synthesis that persists today.
