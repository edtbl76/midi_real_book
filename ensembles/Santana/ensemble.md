# Ensemble: Santana

## Context

Santana formed in San Francisco in 1966 around guitarist Carlos Santana, fusing Afro-Cuban and Brazilian percussion with Chicago blues guitar and San Francisco psychedelia in a way that had no real precedent; their electrifying set at Woodstock in August 1969 — before their debut album was even released — made them an overnight sensation. The band's lineup shifted constantly across five decades, with Carlos as the sole constant, moving through early Latin-rock (Greg Rolie, Michael Shrieve, José Areas), jazz-fusion explorations of the 1970s and 1980s, and the massive commercial comeback of Supernatural (1999). What ties every era together is Carlos Santana's guitar voice: a singing, violin-like sustain shaped by heavy string vibrato and compressed midrange, a pentatonic vocabulary deeply rooted in blues and flamenco, and an instinct for melodic space that locks into the layered percussion rather than competing with it.

## Alphonso Johnson

Alphonso Johnson was born February 2, 1951, in Philadelphia, where he began on upright bass before switching to electric in his late teens; early studies with former Duke Ellington bassist John Lamb at the Philadelphia Music Academy shaped his melodic, lyrical approach. He first gained wide notice as a member of Weather Report from 1973 to 1976, appearing on Mysterious Traveller, Tale Spinnin', and Black Market — albums that established the fretless bass as a lead voice in jazz fusion. Johnson joined Santana in the mid-1980s, playing on Beyond Appearances (1985), Blues for Salvador (1987), and Spirits Dancing in the Flesh (1990), bringing the same fluid legato feel he developed in Weather Report. Outside those two bands he recorded with Billy Cobham, Phil Collins (Face Value, 1981), and Bobby and the Midnites, and later taught music at CSU Northridge.

## Carlos Santana

Carlos Humberto Santana Barragán was born July 20, 1947, in Autlán de Navarro, Jalisco, Mexico; his father was a mariachi violinist, and Carlos absorbed both Mexican folk melody and American blues from radio and records, teaching himself guitar in Tijuana before the family moved to San Francisco in 1961. He founded the Santana Blues Band in 1966, and by the time the band played Woodstock in 1969 — delivering a volcanic forty-five-minute set that left a global audience stunned — he had already developed the signature sound that would define him for the next five decades: a smooth, singing sustain achieved through a fierce left-hand vibrato, a pentatonic vocabulary soaked in blues and Latin melody, and a tone shaped by the interaction between a hot amplifier and a guitar's natural resonance. In the early 1980s he became one of the first major artists to endorse PRS Guitars (the PRS Santana models have remained his primary instrument ever since), though he played a Gibson SG through the band's first three albums and continues to return to it. Santana was inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1998 and won eight Grammy Awards in a single night for Supernatural (1999), one of the best-selling albums in history.

## Alex Acuna

Alejandro Neciosup Acuña was born December 12, 1944, in Pativilca, Peru, into a family of musicians; he was playing in local bands by age ten and at eighteen was recruited by Pérez Prado for his Latin big band. After moving to Puerto Rico in 1966 — where he studied classical percussion at the Conservatory under Pablo Casals's direction — he relocated to Las Vegas in 1974 and became a sought-after session player, gigging with Elvis Presley and Diana Ross before being recruited by Weather Report. From 1975 to 1977, Acuña served as both drummer and percussionist for Weather Report, playing on Black Market (1976) and Heavy Weather (1977) — the band's best-selling album — and his ability to handle both drum kit and Afro-Cuban hand percussion simultaneously made him uniquely valuable. After Weather Report, he settled in Los Angeles as a prolific session musician, appearing on recordings with U2, Paul McCartney, Joni Mitchell, Chick Corea, and Carlos Santana, and won the Modern Drummer readers poll for Best Latin/Brazilian Percussionist fifteen consecutive years.

## Joe Zawinul

Josef Erich Zawinul was born July 7, 1932, in Vienna, Austria, and studied at the Vienna Conservatory before emigrating to the United States in 1959 on a Berklee scholarship; within weeks of arriving, he had joined Maynard Ferguson's band, and within a year he was playing with Cannonball Adderley, for whom he wrote "Mercy, Mercy, Mercy" (1966), one of the era's defining jazz-funk hits. His contributions to Miles Davis's electric period — including compositions and arrangements on In a Silent Way (1969) and Bitches Brew (1970) — placed him at the center of jazz fusion's origin story, and in 1970 he co-founded Weather Report with Wayne Shorter, leading the band until it dissolved in 1986. An early adopter of the ARP 2600 synthesizer (acquired in 1971), Zawinul shaped the sound of electric jazz keyboards through two decades of constant instrument experimentation, and his composition "Birdland" has become one of the most performed pieces in modern jazz. His connection to Santana came through Carlos's guest appearance on Weather Report's final album, This Is This! (1986), and Zawinul's production of Salif Keita's Amen (1991), which also featured Carlos; Zawinul died September 11, 2007, in Vienna.

## Wayne Shorter

Wayne Shorter was born August 25, 1933, in Newark, New Jersey, and majored in music education at New York University before joining Art Blakey's Jazz Messengers in 1959, where he quickly became the band's primary composer and one of its defining improvisers. In 1964 he joined Miles Davis's Second Great Quintet, contributing compositions including "Nefertiti," "ESP," "Fall," and "Sanctuary" that helped define one of the most celebrated periods in jazz history; it was with Davis that he took up soprano saxophone, an instrument that thereafter became as central to his sound as tenor. His three 1964 Blue Note recordings — Night Dreamer, Juju, and Speak No Evil — are considered definitive examples of the post-bop Blue Note sound: sophisticated harmonic structures, strong melodic writing, and exceptional control. In 1970, he co-founded Weather Report with Joe Zawinul, and the band ran sixteen years through continuous reinvention; after it dissolved in 1986, Shorter spent decades leading critically acclaimed quartet recordings and receiving twelve Grammy Awards. His Santana collaboration includes playing on The Swing of Delight (1980) — Carlos's album with Miles Davis alumni — and an extensive 1988 international tour documented on Live at the Montreux Jazz Festival 1988; Shorter died March 2, 2023.

## Manolo Badrena

Manolo Badrena was born March 17, 1952, in San Juan, Puerto Rico, and grew up immersed in Caribbean rhythm before pursuing formal conservatory training in San Juan; as a teenager he absorbed both Afro-Caribbean traditions and U.S. popular music, developing the polyrhythmic fluency that would define his studio career. He joined Weather Report in 1976 after keyboardist Joe Zawinul invited him to record what became Heavy Weather (1977), one of the best-selling jazz albums ever made, and he also appears on Mr. Gone (1978). Outside Weather Report, Badrena's session resume has spanned jazz, pop, rock, and Latin music — credits include recordings with the Rolling Stones, Joni Mitchell, Spyro Gyra, Art Blakey, Talking Heads, Blondie, Steve Khan, and Bill Evans — and in 1979 he released a solo album, Manolo, on A&M. He has led the Latin jazz group Trio Mundo and worked with the Zawinul Syndicate after Weather Report's dissolution; his connection to Santana's orbit comes through the shared Weather Report network rather than direct Santana band membership.
