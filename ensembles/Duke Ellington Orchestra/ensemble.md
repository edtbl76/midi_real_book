# Ensemble: Duke Ellington Orchestra

## Context

The Duke Ellington Orchestra was one of the most musically sophisticated and long-lived ensembles in American music, active under Ellington's leadership from the early 1920s until his death in 1974. Ellington treated the orchestra as a living compositional instrument, writing specific pieces to spotlight individual players' voices — a practice that produced concertos, tone poems, and suites tailored to the sounds of Cootie Williams's growl mute, Johnny Hodges's lush alto tone, and Jimmy Blanton's melodic bass. The ensemble's recordings from 1939–1942 in particular represent a peak of the swing era, capturing a rhythm section and soloist roster that changed what jazz orchestras could do.

## Jimmy Blanton

Jimmy Blanton revolutionized the role of the jazz bass. Before his arrival in the Ellington Orchestra in 1939, the bass was almost exclusively a time-keeping instrument; Blanton introduced melodic soloing, horn-like phrasing, and an assured walking authority that made the instrument a full participant in ensemble conversation. He recorded a series of celebrated piano-bass duets with Ellington and appeared on the orchestra's landmark 1940 sessions. He died of tuberculosis in 1942 at age 23, having transformed the instrument in roughly two years.

## Barney Kessel

Barney Kessel was not a core member of the Ellington Orchestra. He was one of the most important jazz guitarists of the postwar era, whose orbit overlapped with many of the same swing-to-bop players who surrounded Ellington. He worked extensively with Jazz at the Philharmonic, recorded a celebrated series of albums for Contemporary Records with his own trio, and was one of the busiest studio musicians in Los Angeles (a key figure in the informal circle later called the Wrecking Crew). His guitar style bridged swing rhythm-section vocabulary with bebop single-note fluency, and his trio recordings with Ray Brown and Shelly Manne (the Poll Winners sessions) are among the most influential small-group jazz guitar records of the 1950s.

## Buddy Rich

Buddy Rich was not a core member of the Ellington Orchestra. He was one of the most technically accomplished drummers in jazz history, known for his breathtaking speed, crisp snare work, and the ability to drive a big band with almost no physical effort apparent. His primary affiliations were with the Tommy Dorsey Orchestra in the early 1940s and his own big band, which he reformed multiple times from 1946 through the 1980s. His 1966–1970 period with his big band produced some of the most widely circulated examples of modern big-band drumming on record.

## Duke Ellington

Duke Ellington was one of the most consequential American composers and bandleaders of the twentieth century. He led his orchestra — in various configurations — for nearly fifty years, from the early 1920s until his death in 1974. As a pianist he was economical and orchestrally minded, shaping his voicings around the specific sounds of his players rather than pursuing a signature solo style. As a composer he produced an enormous body of work spanning dance-hall standards, extended concert suites, film scores, and sacred music. His partnership with arranger and composer Billy Strayhorn, which began in 1939, deepened and extended the orchestra's musical range significantly.

## Johnny Hodges

Johnny Hodges was the defining solo voice of the Ellington Orchestra for nearly forty years. His alto saxophone tone — lush, full, with a wide vibrato that never slid into sentimentality — was one of the most immediately recognizable sounds in jazz. Ellington built compositions around Hodges's sound, and the orchestra's most lyrical recordings are inseparable from his playing. He spent almost his entire career with Ellington (1928–1970), with a hiatus from 1951 to 1955 when he led his own small group. He died in 1970, still a member of the orchestra, with Ellington reportedly unable to replace him.

## Cootie Williams

Cootie Williams was one of the most distinctive trumpet voices in jazz — specifically because of his mastery of the plunger mute and growl technique, which allowed him to produce a range of vocal-sounding inflections that Ellington exploited fully in composition. He joined the orchestra in 1929 and became such an important part of its identity that when he left to join Benny Goodman in 1940, Raymond Scott composed a satirical tribute called "When Cootie Left the Duke." He led his own bands through the 1940s and 1950s before returning to Ellington in 1962, remaining with the orchestra until after Ellington's death in 1974.
