# Ensemble: studio groove

## Context

The New York session world of the late 1960s and 1970s produced a cohesive community of R&B, soul, and funk players who appeared across hundreds of records — from Aretha Franklin's Atlantic sessions to Steely Dan's studio records. Chuck Rainey, Cornell Dupree, Eric Gale, Bernard Purdie, and Richard Tee formed the core of this session scene, recording together on countless dates. King Curtis was both a session leader and a solo artist whose saxophone defined the Atlantic Records soul sound until his murder in 1971.

## Chuck Rainey

Chuck Rainey is one of the most recorded session bassists in history, appearing on thousands of records across R&B, soul, funk, jazz, and pop. His peak period in the late 1960s through 1970s found him recording for Aretha Franklin, King Curtis, Steely Dan, Quincy Jones, and hundreds of other artists. Rainey's bass playing is defined by his rhythmic precision, chromatic approach to passing tones, and an unfailing groove instinct that works equally in slow soul ballads and fast funk tracks.

## Eric Gale

Eric Gale was a Brooklyn-born guitarist whose career in jazz, R&B, and session work spanned from the late 1950s through the 1980s. Like Dupree, he worked with King Curtis and Aretha Franklin in the late 1960s, and was a core member of Stuff. His playing has a more jazz-influenced character than Dupree's — lighter touch, more chord-melody, and a smoother, less blues-raw attack. Gale's session versatility made him equally at home on sophisticated Creed Taylor productions and raw Atlantic soul dates.

## Bernard Purdie

Bernard "Pretty" Purdie is a drummer from Maryland who is considered by many to be the greatest soul and R&B drummer who ever lived. His signature "Purdie shuffle" — a triplet-based half-time feel with ghost notes — became the foundational groove for countless recordings. Purdie has claimed an extraordinarily wide (and sometimes disputed) range of session credits, but his documented work with King Curtis, Aretha Franklin, Steely Dan, and others is indisputably foundational to American popular music.

## Richard Tee

Richard Tee was a Jamaican-American pianist and organist who was a founding member of Stuff and one of New York's most in-demand session keyboardists from the late 1960s through the 1980s. His playing combines gospel-church chord voicings, soul organ feel, and jazz harmonic sophistication. Tee worked with Aretha Franklin, Paul Simon, Paul McCartney, and countless others, and his keyboard style is deeply rooted in the African-American church tradition that underlies soul music.

## King Curtis

King Curtis (Curtis Ousley) was a Texas-born tenor saxophonist who became one of the most important figures in New York's soul and R&B world from the late 1950s until his murder in 1971. He was Aretha Franklin's musical director at Atlantic Records, led his own King Curtis and the Kingpins band, and appeared as a soloist and session player on hundreds of records. His saxophone tone — warm, direct, with a bluesy vocabulary rooted in rhythm and blues — defined the soul saxophone sound of the Atlantic era.
